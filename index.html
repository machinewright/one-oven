    How it plans

    An oven can only be at one temperature, and before a dish goes in it should have been at the right heat for a while (I assume 15 minutes). A dish that is already in when the temperature changes is assumed to be fine at both temperatures (the planner only does that within what you allowed, or as a listed compromise), and a dish stays in one oven from start to finish (with two ovens, you can tie a dish to one of them, say if the turkey only fits in the big one). Shelves, hob rings and the cook's hands are limited too. The planner counts the day in five-minute steps and tries start times for every dish. Among the plans that respect those limits it picks the one where food waits the least after it's done and the oven changes temperature the fewest times. For a small menu it checks every possibility; for a big one it searches for a fixed amount of work and says so under the plan (the same menu always gives the same plan).

    When nothing fits as written, it doesn't just give up. It's allowed three kinds of compromise, each with a price: letting a dish wait longer than you said, cooking it up to 20 °C off its recipe temperature, or leaving it out (to make ahead, or to cook in the microwave or air fryer). It picks the plan with the smallest total price and lists every compromise at the top, so you can decide whether to accept it or change your menu. A dish marked exact temperature never gets the temperature compromise; use that for cakes, pastry and Yorkshire puddings. If one dish matters more to you than the rest, mark it matters most: its waiting and its compromises then count four times as much, so the others give way first (in the worst case another dish is left out, and the plan lists that too). A marked dish can still need a compromise when sparing it would cost the others even more.

    What it doesn't know: whether a particular dish copes with a hotter oven, how full your oven really is, or whether your turkey is cooked. Check meat with a thermometer: the UK Food Standards Agency says poultry is done when it's steaming hot all the way through, there's no pink meat left and the juices run clear (FSA); in the US the guide is 165 °F (74 °C) for all poultry, measured in the thickest part (the FoodSafety.gov chart, as reproduced by University of Maine Extension).

    The example is olive magazine's published Christmas timeline, entered step by step; the planner reproduces its oven times to the minute. Durations the page doesn't give (frying the sprouts, making the gravy) are my estimates. The code is on GitHub, with tests that check the planner against a brute-force search on hundreds of small menus.
